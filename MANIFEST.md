# Euclid

*Expertise can be made explicit.*

We do not know when Euclid was born or where, when he died or where, whether he married, what he looked like, or whether he was one man rather than a school publishing under a shared name. Thomas Heath, who produced the standard English edition, permitted himself a single sentence of biography in 1908: "All that is certainly known is that Euclid taught and founded a school at Alexandria." What survives instead is 465 propositions in thirteen books, ordered so that nothing depends on anything that follows it. The ordering is the only biography available, and it has proven sufficient.

The rest comes from Proclus, a Neoplatonist writing around 450 CE, seven centuries downstream. Proclus places Euclid under Ptolemy I, younger than Plato's pupils and older than Archimedes, and reports that when the king asked whether there might be some shorter road through geometry than the Elements, Euclid told him there was no royal road. The same exchange is elsewhere attributed to Menaechmus and Alexander, which is the usual fate of a good line.

Alexandria was thirty years old when Ptolemy took it as his capital, and the Ptolemies spent lavishly to make it the place where Greek learning was collected rather than merely produced. The Mouseion held the scholars and the Library held the scrolls, and the two projects were the same project: gather what is scattered, put it in an order, make it retrievable. The Elements does to theorems what the Library did to literature. It is a cataloguing achievement before it is a mathematical one, and its author would not have found that description insulting.

The material was already there. Thales is credited with introducing demonstration around 600 BCE, though the Greek verb his commentators use, *apodeiknumi*, is loose enough to mean anything from "prove" to "point at." The Pythagoreans made number the substrate of reality and then discovered, presumably to their considerable distress, that the diagonal of a square cannot be written as a ratio of whole numbers relative to its side. A school whose central commitment was that all things stand in ratio had found two lengths that do not. Geometry could not be reduced to the arithmetic of integers, and the theory of proportion that everyone had been using silently assumed it could.

Eudoxus repaired it in the mid-fourth century with a definition that ought to feel much more modern than it is. Rather than asking what common measure two magnitudes share, he asked how they behave against every integer multiple: A is to B as C is to D when, for all whole numbers m and n, mA compares to nB exactly as mC compares to nD. The definition never mentions a common measure, so it does not care whether one exists. It survives verbatim as Book V, it is the deepest thing in the Elements, and it works by quantifying over an infinite family of comparisons roughly two millennia before anyone had a notation for doing so.

Hippocrates of Chios had already written an Elements a century before Euclid's, now lost. Plato's Academy supplied Theaetetus on the regular solids and irrationals and Eudoxus on proportion and exhaustion. Euclid's contribution was not discovery. It was deciding what depended on what.

Book I opens with twenty-three definitions, five postulates, and five common notions, and then never appeals to anything else. The definitions are the weakest part and everyone knows it: a point is that which has no part, a line is breadthless length. These do not tell you how to make a point or recognize one. They are glosses, orienting the reader, and the logical work is done entirely by the postulates. Three of the five license constructions, which is to say they define the reach of straightedge and compass. The fourth asserts that all right angles are equal. The fifth is visibly a stranger among them, longer than the other four combined, asserting an intersection rather than permitting a construction, and requiring the reader to compare two angles against a standard rather than simply look. Mathematicians noticed within a few centuries that it did not belong, and spent the next two thousand years trying to evict it.

The forty-eight propositions of Book I build triangle congruence, parallels, and parallelograms, and arrive at the Pythagorean theorem and its converse. Book II states algebraic identities as facts about rectangles, which reads as perverse until one remembers there was no algebraic notation to state them in; geometry was the only available language for general quantitative claims. Books III and IV do circles and inscribed figures. Book V is Eudoxus. Book VI applies him to similar figures. Books VII through IX are arithmetic, and contain the Euclidean algorithm, the infinitude of the primes, and the formula for even perfect numbers. Books XI through XIII go into three dimensions and end by constructing the five regular solids and proving there are no others.

Book X is 115 propositions classifying irrational magnitudes, the longest and least read book in the work. It exists because Book XIII needs it. The edges and diagonals of the regular solids are irrational in several distinct ways, and the classification is the machinery required to say which. Nobody reads Book X for pleasure. It is what a foundation costs when the thing being founded is more interesting than the foundation.

What the arrangement buys is locality. Because the dependency graph is acyclic, a proposition can be checked against its proof and the handful of results that proof invokes, without reading the other four hundred. Disagreement is forced into one of two places: you reject a postulate, or you reject a step. There is nowhere else to stand. This is a considerable achievement and it is the thing the Elements actually taught.

It is also not quite true, because Euclid assumes more than he states. He assumes that three points on a line admit an order, that one lies between the other two. He assumes that certain figures intersect. The most conspicuous case is Proposition 1 of Book I, the very first thing in the work, which constructs an equilateral triangle by drawing two circles and taking a point where they cross. Euclid does not argue that they cross. He draws the figure and proceeds. The foundational text of Western deductive rigor has a hole in its first proposition, and for two thousand years the standard response of the educated reader was to look at the picture and agree.

Proposition 4 has a different kind of problem. Euclid proves side-angle-side congruence by picking one triangle up and setting it on the other. Nothing in the postulates licenses moving a figure, and motion is an odd thing to attribute to objects that have no parts and no breadth. He uses the method sparingly, in the manner of a man who knows he is getting away with something. Later geometers took side-angle-side as an axiom outright, which is the usual way such debts are settled.

The text survived by being copied. The oldest Greek manuscripts postdate Euclid by more than a thousand years and descend mostly from Theon of Alexandria's fourth-century edition. Arabic translators took it up in the ninth century and extended it rather than merely keeping it alive. Thabit ibn Qurra generalized the Pythagorean theorem, Omar Khayyam classified cubics, and both engaged directly with the fifth postulate. Latin translations from the Arabic reached Europe in the twelfth century, the first printed edition appeared in Venice in 1482, and Clavius's 1574 edition went wherever the Jesuits went, which was nearly everywhere. Heiberg produced the critical edition in the 1880s and Heath the English one in 1908.

The assault on the fifth postulate is the longest failure in mathematics and the most productive. Ptolemy attempted a proof; Proclus reports it was fallacious, then offered his own, which was circular. Thabit assumed that points equidistant from a line form a line, which is the postulate wearing a hat. Every attempt for two millennia smuggled in an equivalent assumption, usually without the author noticing.

Saccheri, in 1733, came closest and understood least. He assumed the postulate false and worked forward, deriving consequence after consequence in search of a contradiction. He eliminated the obtuse case cleanly. The acute case would not break. He kept going, generating results that struck him as repugnant but were not contradictory, until he declared victory on the strength of an argument that does not hold. His book is titled *Euclid Freed of Every Flaw*. He had spent it doing non-Euclidean geometry, correctly, and had mistaken the theorems for symptoms.

Gauss worked it out privately and published nothing, apparently anticipating the reception. Lobachevsky published in 1829 and Bolyai in 1832, and the answer turned out to be that the postulate is independent: there are consistent geometries in which the other four hold and it fails, so no proof from the other four could ever have existed. Two thousand years of effort had been directed at a theorem that is not one.

The discovery raised the obvious follow-up. If the fifth postulate was not self-evident, what about the four that had looked safe, and what about the assumptions Euclid never wrote down at all? Pasch, in 1882, identified the betweenness assumptions and supplied the axiom that bears his name: a line entering a triangle through one side leaves through another. Hilbert finished the job in 1899, sorting the axioms of Euclidean geometry into incidence, order, congruence, parallels, and continuity, promoting side-angle-side to an axiom so that nobody would have to move a triangle again, and then demonstrating the independence of each axiom by exhibiting a model in which it alone fails. Euclid's gaps took twenty-two centuries to close, and closing them produced the template for every axiomatization since.

None of which is a criticism, or at least not one that would have landed. Euclid was not attempting to satisfy Hilbert. He was attempting to establish what rests on what, and he did it well enough that the result was still worth repairing two thousand years later, which is not a fate that befalls most books.

The influence that matters is not geometric. It is the discovery that an argument can be built so that its assumptions are visible and its dependencies are traceable, and that a reader who accepts the beginning is compelled to accept the end or else point to the step where the compulsion fails. Spinoza wrote his ethics in that form. Newton claimed the same standing for physics in his title. When the twentieth century axiomatized set theory and algebra and analysis, it was doing what Alexandria had done, at greater length and with the gaps filled in.

# Ramon Llull

*Expertise can be mechanized.*

Ramon Llull was born around 1232 on Majorca, an island the Aragonese had taken from the Moors within living memory, and grew up in a frontier society where Christians, Muslims, and Jews lived close enough to argue. He was a courtier and a troubadour poet with a wife, children, and a post as seneschal, and by his own account a man of no particular seriousness, until he was about thirty and Christ appeared to him on the cross on five successive nights.

He came away with three objectives: to write the best book in the world against the errors of unbelievers, to found monasteries where missionaries would learn the languages of the people they intended to convert, and to be martyred. He achieved the second, arguably the third, and spent forty years on the first.

The first objective contained a problem he could not argue his way around. He wanted to convince Muslims and Jews of Christian doctrine. Scripture was unavailable to him as evidence, since the authority of Christian scripture was the thing in dispute. Faith was unavailable, since faith was the conclusion. He needed premises his opponents already held and a procedure that would carry them somewhere they did not want to go, and the procedure had to be one they could check rather than one they had to trust.

He spent nine years preparing. He learned Arabic, reportedly by buying a Muslim slave and having the man teach him, an arrangement that ended when the slave attacked him and then hanged himself in his cell. He read Islamic philosophy and Christian theology and looked for the common ground. In 1274, on Mount Randa, he received the structure of the thing in a vision.

The Art was not a book, though he wrote several dozen describing it. It was a procedure. It began with nine Dignities, the attributes that all three faiths predicate of God: Goodness, Greatness, Eternity, Power, Wisdom, Will, Virtue, Truth, Glory. A Muslim would grant every one. So would a Jew. The common ground was not a matter of persuasion; it was a matter of inventory.

Llull assigned the Dignities letters, B through K, and built tables and rotating concentric disks that generated the letter pairs mechanically. BC, BD, BE, and onward. Each pair was to be read as a proposition or a question: Goodness is great, or is Goodness great? The Art then supplied rules for sorting the results into the necessary, the possible, and the impossible. Turn the wheels, enumerate the combinations, evaluate each, and you have surveyed the space of what can be said about God without having relied on anyone's testimony.

He extended it considerably: nine relational principles, nine subjects ascending from God through angel and heaven and man down to the merely instrumental, and rules for crossing between categories. The mature system is elaborate enough that mastering it was a course of study, which is one reason it did not spread.

The apologetic payload was the Trinity. If God's attributes are real and distinct rather than nominal, the relations among them generate internal structure, and Llull believed the structure could be shown to be Trinitarian by combination alone. The strategy is clean even where the execution is not: take premises your opponent has already conceded, apply a procedure he can verify step by step, and arrive at the doctrine he rejects.

He promoted it for the rest of his life with a persistence that verges on the pathological. He lectured at Paris. He petitioned popes and kings. He wrote more than 250 works in Catalan, Latin, and Arabic, and he founded the language school at Miramar. He crossed to North Africa three times to preach, and tradition holds that the third crossing killed him, stoned by a crowd in Tunis around 1315. He was beatified and never canonized.

The Art converted nobody. It was too hard to learn, too controversial to teach, and aimed at an audience with no reason to sit through it. What survived was the machinery, and it survived in company Llull would have deplored. Agrippa turned the combinatorial method to occult ends in the sixteenth century. Bruno built enormous memory theaters on Lullian principles and was burned in 1600, with the Art among the reasons he was interesting to the Inquisition.

Then Leibniz found it. In 1666, at nineteen, he published the *Dissertatio de Arte Combinatoria*, credited Llull directly, and proposed to fix him. Where Llull had nine Dignities fixed by theology, Leibniz wanted an alphabet of human thought, the complete set of primitives from which every concept is composed. Where Llull turned wheels, Leibniz wanted a calculus. Everything Leibniz spent his life failing to build is recognizably an attempt to repair the Majorcan's machine.

The specific defect Leibniz was repairing is worth naming, because it is the defect that outlasted both of them. Llull's wheels generate propositions mechanically and then stop. Evaluating what they produce requires a trained practitioner at every step, deciding what BC means in this context and whether it is necessary or merely possible. Generation was automatic; judgment was not, and judgment was the entire cost. The Art produced candidates faster than any human could assess them, which made it useless, because assessment was the bottleneck and the Art did not touch it.

He did not build a formal system. His procedure was welded to theological commitments that no formal system could carry, his rules were inadequate, and his goal was conversion rather than proof. What he saw, and saw first, is that the space of propositions has a structure, that the structure can be enumerated rather than intuited, and that a device can do the enumerating. Seven centuries later the wheels are faster and the bottleneck has moved, but a man turning a disk to see what proposition comes up next would recognize what is happening.

# Leibniz

*Expertise should be calculation.*

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz was born in Leipzig in 1646, two years before the Peace of Westphalia, and lost his father at six. What his father left was a library, and the boy taught himself Latin out of it by setting a German text beside its translation and working out the correspondence. He entered the university at fourteen and was offered a professorship at Altdorf at twenty, which he declined. He never held an academic post. He spent his working life as a functionary of the House of Hanover, cataloguing the library, advising on law, supervising the drainage of silver mines in the Harz, and failing to complete a commissioned history of his employers' family.

Around the edges of this he invented the calculus, designed a machine that could multiply, proposed binary arithmetic, articulated the principle of sufficient reason and the identity of indiscernibles, built a metaphysics out of monads, argued that this is the best of all possible worlds, and corresponded with several hundred scholars across Europe. He was among the last people who could plausibly claim to know everything, and he spent his life trying to build the thing that would make knowing everything unnecessary.

The project had two halves. The *characteristica universalis* was to be a notation in which every concept was written as a composition of primitives, unambiguous, international, and independent of any natural language. The *calculus ratiocinator* was to be the procedure that operated on it. Together they would convert reasoning into arithmetic. He put it plainly, and the sentence has outlived nearly everything else he wrote: "The only way to rectify our reasonings is to make them as tangible as those of the Mathematicians, so that we can find our error at a glance, and when there are disputes among persons, we can simply say: Let us calculate, without further ado, to see who is right."

He never built it. The papers contain notations, partial analyses, proposed primitives, and letters about the plan, extending across fifty years and stopping short in every direction.

The reasons are worth separating, because one of them is not about Leibniz. He was busy, and the project competed with the mines and the genealogy and the calculus priority dispute with Newton. He was also attempting something with no termination condition. Analyzing a concept into its primitives requires knowing when the analysis is finished, and nothing in the program supplies a criterion. You decompose until you reach something that feels primitive, and the feeling is not a proof. Leibniz believed the primitives were innate, planted by God in every rational mind, which would have supplied the stopping rule had it been true. It also meant that any disagreement about which concepts were primitive fell outside the reach of the calculus meant to settle disagreements. The instrument for ending disputes could not be built without first settling a dispute.

What he built instead was influential out of proportion to its completeness. The calculus notation is his and is still in use, which is more than can be said for Newton's. The vision of reasoning as symbol manipulation ran through Boole, who mechanized a fragment of it algebraically, and through Frege, whose *Begriffsschrift* is a working piece of the universal characteristic even though Frege arrived at it independently. He distinguished the form of an argument from its content, which is the precondition for treating logic as syntax. He defined identity by indiscernibility and definition by substitutability, and both definitions survived.

He also invented binary arithmetic and read it theologically, one standing for God and zero for nothing, all numbers generated from the two as creation was generated from nothing. The theology has not aged and the insight underneath it has: any content can be encoded in a minimal alphabet, and computation is what you get when symbols in that alphabet are transformed by fixed rules. His stepped drum mechanism did multiplication and division, which Pascal's machine could not, and he speculated about machines that would operate on propositions rather than numbers. The hardware of his century could not hold the idea, but the idea was fully formed.

He died in Hanover in November 1716. His employer had by then become George I of Great Britain and did not attend the funeral, nor did anyone from the Royal Society, of which Leibniz was a member and had briefly been the most distinguished. The history of the House of Hanover reached the year 1005. The universal characteristic existed in fragments in a drawer. The academies he had spent decades lobbying into existence were flourishing and credited him with little.

The dream was worth more than its author got for it. Leibniz established that formalization of all knowledge was a coherent thing to want, and that the wanting had a structure: a language for expressing claims and a procedure for deriving them. Everyone who has since spoken of formal languages and formal methods has been using vocabulary he assembled to describe a machine he could not construct.
# Frege

*Expertise can be formalized.*

In 1879 a mathematics lecturer at Jena published an eighty-eight page pamphlet whose title promised a formula language of pure thought modeled on that of arithmetic. Almost nobody read it. Its notation was two-dimensional, built from horizontal content strokes and vertical negation strokes and branching conditionals, and it was close to impossible to typeset. Reviewers who managed to work through it were unimpressed. It contains the largest single advance in logic between Aristotle and the present.

Gottlob Frege was born in Wismar on the Baltic in 1848, the son of a girls' school headmaster, and studied at Jena and Göttingen before returning to Jena for the rest of his life. He rose eventually to honorary ordinary professor, a title that conveys less than it sounds like and paid accordingly. He had few students and no chair. He died in 1925 with his reputation confined to a handful of people who had understood what he did.

What logic had been before him was Aristotle with refinements. The syllogism handled "all A are B" and "some A are B" and had handled them for two thousand years. Boole had made a real advance in 1847 by treating classes algebraically, so that logical arguments could be checked by calculation, and De Morgan had extended the treatment to relations. But Boolean algebra breaks on the sentences mathematics actually needs. It cannot express "for every number there exists a greater one," because it has no way to say that the second quantity depends on the first. Every interesting statement in analysis has that structure and none of them could be written down.

Frege's solution was to abandon subject and predicate for function and argument. A concept is a function from objects to truth values. A quantifier binds a variable and states the scope of a generality. "All men are mortal" becomes: for all x, if x is a man then x is mortal, with the x explicit and the dependency visible. Quantifiers can then be nested, and the order can be varied, and the difference between "for every number there is a greater one" and "there is a number greater than every number" becomes a matter of which symbol you write first. The whole of predicate logic falls out. The notation was discarded within a generation and translated into Peano's linear symbols, which is a fact about printing rather than about logic. The architecture underneath is unchanged today.

He also stated his axioms explicitly, listed his rules of inference, and conducted proofs in which each step was licensed by a named rule. This was not the custom.

The point of building the logic was to use it. Frege held that arithmetic was a branch of logic, that the truths of number were logical truths requiring no appeal to intuition or observation, and that this could be demonstrated by deriving arithmetic inside a sufficiently powerful logical system. He had just built the system. The remaining work was the derivation.

*Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* appeared in 1884 and is written almost entirely in prose, which is why it is the only one of his books that is widely read. It disposes of the alternatives first. Numbers are not properties of physical heaps, since the same heap is one deck and fifty-two cards. They are not products of Kantian intuition, since nothing in intuition distinguishes 999,999 from a million. They are not psychological, since the number seven does not vary with the mood of the person thinking about it. What remains is that a number is a logical object: the class of all classes that can be put in one-to-one correspondence with a given class. Correspondence is definable logically, so number is too. The book also introduced the distinction between sense and reference, the principle that a word means something only in the context of a sentence, and a demolition of psychologism in logic that has never needed redoing.

The formal derivation took another nine years. Volume one of the *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* appeared in 1893, volume two in 1903. The system rested on a small set of axioms including Basic Law V, which says that two functions have the same extension exactly when they agree on every argument. It looks harmless. It is the sentence that says every well-defined condition carves out a class.

In June 1902, with volume two in press, Bertrand Russell wrote to point out that the condition "is not a member of itself" carves out a class that is a member of itself if and only if it is not.

Frege added an appendix. It opens: "A scientist can hardly meet with anything more undesirable than to have the foundation give way just as the work is finished. I was placed in this position by a letter from Mr. Bertrand Russell, just as the printing of the second volume was nearing completion." He proposed a weakened form of Basic Law V, and the weakened form was shown later to fail as well, in a manner he did not live to see. Twenty-five years of work rested on one axiom out of a handful, and the axiom was the one that made the others do anything.

He published little afterward. He grew embittered, and the diaries of his final year record antisemitic and anti-democratic views. He died in Bad Kleinen in 1925.

The recovery began in the 1950s, when logicians went back to the texts and found that most of what they were doing had been done. The *Begriffsschrift* is the boundary: logic before it is a subject with a fixed inventory of valid forms, and logic after it is a subject with a language. Type theory, model theory, proof theory, and the semantics of programming languages all sit downstream of a pamphlet nobody bought.

The failure did work of its own. Basic Law V is what naive set formation looks like when written down carefully enough to be refuted, and the axiomatizations by Zermelo and Fraenkel are responses to the hole Frege exposed by falling into it. What he established, and what survived the collapse of what he was trying to establish it for, is that mathematical reasoning can be reduced to fixed rules applied to explicit symbols, and that an informal proof is in principle translatable into a form where every step is checkable by inspection. Whether anything other than a person could do the inspecting was not a question available to him.

# Russell and Whitehead

*Expertise requires exhaustive rigor.*

The proof that 1 + 1 = 2 appears on page 379 of the first volume of *Principia Mathematica*, and the authors note that the proposition is occasionally useful. Everything before it is the apparatus required to make the statement mean something. The remark is the only joke in three volumes and it is not entirely a joke.

Bertrand Russell was born in 1872 into a family that had supplied two Prime Ministerships, orphaned young, and raised by a grandmother in conditions of considerable propriety and no warmth. He went to Trinity, read mathematics and philosophy, lived to ninety-seven, married four times, campaigned against three wars and one nuclear posture, went to prison for it twice, and won a Nobel Prize in literature. The work that outlasted all of it was done in his thirties with his old teacher.

Alfred North Whitehead was born in 1861 in Ramsgate, the son of an Anglican clergyman, went to Trinity, and stayed there teaching mathematics for a quarter century. At sixty-three he moved to Harvard and became a metaphysician, which is an unusual second act. He died in 1947.

Russell came to the project through Peano. At the Paris congress of 1900 he encountered Peano's symbolic notation, saw immediately that it was more usable than Frege's and could be pushed further than Peano had pushed it, and spent the following months working on the logical foundations of arithmetic in a state of high excitement. Then, in the spring of 1901, he found the contradiction.

The naive principle is that any property determines the set of things having it. Most sets do not contain themselves; the set of cats is not a cat. Consider the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. If it contains itself it does not, and if it does not it does. The argument is four sentences long, requires no technical apparatus, and destroys the foundations of two different systems.

Russell's eventual repair was the theory of types. Objects are stratified: individuals at the bottom, sets of individuals above them, sets of those above that. Membership is only defined between adjacent levels, so no set can contain itself, so the question that generates the paradox cannot be asked. The contradiction is not answered. It is rendered ungrammatical.

The price was steep and Russell knew it. A simple hierarchy was not enough; he needed a ramified one, distinguishing sets defined by reference to a totality from sets defined without such reference, and the ramification broke ordinary mathematics. To repair the damage he added the axiom of reducibility, asserting that every set has an equivalent at the lowest available level, and the axiom of infinity, asserting that infinitely many things exist. Neither is a truth of logic by any reading. The thesis had been that mathematics reduces to logic. What the volumes actually demonstrate is that mathematics reduces to logic plus two assumptions that are not logical, which is a different and much weaker claim.

Set against that, the achievement is difficult to overstate and easy to misdescribe. *Principia Mathematica*, in three volumes between 1910 and 1913, derived propositional logic, predicate logic, the theory of classes and relations, cardinal and ordinal arithmetic, and the real numbers, from a stated handful of axioms by stated rules, with the derivations carried out rather than sketched. Every step is on the page. A fourth volume on geometry was planned and never written; Whitehead had the material and left it.

The labor was extraordinary and it damaged both of them. Russell wrote later that his intellect never fully recovered from the effort, an assessment his subsequent output does not obviously contradict. Whitehead carried more of the technical burden in the later volumes and afterward left the subject entirely. Cambridge University Press estimated it would lose money on the book and required a subvention; the authors covered part of it themselves. They earned, between them, a net loss on the most rigorous document in the history of mathematics.

Reception was respectful and thin. It was cited more than it was read and read more than it was checked. The notation deterred casual readers and the length deterred serious ones, and the philosophical debate settled onto reducibility and infinity, where it stayed.

Then in 1931 Gödel proved that no consistent system capable of arithmetic can prove every arithmetical truth, and that no such system can prove its own consistency. This is not a defect in *Principia*. It is a limit on the enterprise *Principia* was the best available instance of, and Gödel names the book in his title because it was the standard against which any such claim had to be measured. Completeness was never available. The volumes could not have been finished in the sense their authors intended, and the impossibility had nothing to do with how carefully they worked.

What the book proved, in the end, is not what it set out to prove. It established that formalization at full rigor and industrial scale is possible, because here is one. It established simultaneously that doing it by hand is close to unendurable, because here are two first-rate minds who did it once and never again. Both halves of that lesson were necessary. The first told everyone afterward that the thing could be done. The second told them not to do it the way Russell and Whitehead had.

# Hilbert

*Expertise should certify itself.*

Hilbert's tombstone in Göttingen carries two sentences from his 1930 retirement address: *Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen.* We must know, we will know. He had recorded them for radio in Königsberg in September of that year. At a conference in the same city, in the same week, a twenty-four-year-old logician mentioned in discussion that arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove.

David Hilbert was born in 1862 in Königsberg, Kant's city, to a judge and a mother with interests in astronomy. He took his doctorate in 1885 on invariant theory, spent a decade at Königsberg forming the friendships with Minkowski and Hurwitz that set his working style, and moved to Göttingen in 1895, where he stayed and where he made the mathematics department the best in the world. He worked in invariant theory, algebraic number theory, geometry, integral equations, physics, and foundations, and he entered most of those fields by solving the problem the specialists had given up on and then leaving.

The *Grundlagen der Geometrie* of 1899 finished what Euclid started, in the specific sense of writing down what Euclid had assumed without noticing. Hilbert sorted the axioms into incidence, order, congruence, parallels, and continuity, and then did the thing that made the book a template rather than a monograph: he proved each axiom independent by exhibiting a model in which that one fails and the others hold. The technique treats an axiom system as an object of study rather than a description of something, and it requires taking seriously that the primitives mean nothing. Hilbert said so, in the remark everyone quotes, that one must be able to say tables, chairs, and beer mugs in place of points, lines, and planes without the theorems noticing.

In Paris in 1900 he listed twenty-three problems for the coming century. Several concerned foundations. The second asked for a proof that the axioms of arithmetic cannot lead to contradiction. The tenth asked for an algorithm deciding whether a Diophantine equation has solutions. The list is not a set of puzzles; it is a statement of what Hilbert took mathematics to be capable of. Both of those problems assume an answer exists.

The paradoxes arriving around the same time made the assumption urgent. Russell's contradiction had shown that unrestricted set formation is inconsistent, others followed, and the responses fractured. Russell built types, Zermelo axiomatized, and Brouwer proposed intuitionism, which would have preserved consistency by discarding the law of excluded middle and with it most of analysis. Hilbert regarded that cure as worse than the disease and said that taking excluded middle from the mathematician was like taking the telescope from the astronomer or the fist from the boxer.

His program was designed to keep classical mathematics and pay for it. First, formalize completely: every statement in a formal language, every proof a finite string manipulated by explicit rules, checking reduced to symbol comparison requiring no insight whatever. Second, prove the resulting system consistent using only finitary methods, arguments about finite configurations of symbols that even an intuitionist would accept. The infinite would then be licensed by a finite argument, and the licence would be unimpeachable because it presupposed nothing the skeptics denied. Third, establish completeness and decidability: every statement provable or refutable, and an algorithm to say which.

He assembled a school for it. Bernays was the closest collaborator, with Ackermann, von Neumann, and Gentzen among those who contributed, and Göttingen produced a steady stream of results in proof theory through the 1920s. Formalization advanced. Consistency proofs for restricted systems arrived. Gentzen eventually proved arithmetic consistent by transfinite induction up to a specific ordinal, which is a genuine result and is not finitary, and the gap between what was wanted and what was obtained is the whole program in miniature.

Gödel's paper of 1931 removed the second and third components and left the first standing. Consistency cannot be proved by methods formalizable in the system whose consistency is at issue. Completeness is unavailable to any system strong enough to be interesting. Church and Turing closed off decidability five years later.

Hilbert's response is not well documented. Bernays recalled anger. There is no published rebuttal, and no acknowledgment either. He may have taken Gentzen's proof as a partial recovery of what he wanted, and there is an argument that he never fully absorbed what had happened.

What is documented is the destruction of Göttingen, which took about four months. The civil service law of April 1933 removed Jewish academics from their positions and the department emptied: Noether, Courant, Weyl, Bernays, and dozens more, into exile or into worse. At a banquet, Bernhard Rust, the Nazi education minister, asked Hilbert whether the institute had really suffered so much from the departure of the Jews and their friends. Hilbert answered that it had not suffered, since it no longer existed.

He died in Göttingen in February 1943. Around a dozen people came to the funeral. The epitaph was carved anyway, and it asserts something that had been false, and known to be false, for twelve years by the time he died.

The program failed in the form he stated it and the failure was productive in a way that is easy to state and was not obvious in advance. To ask whether a formal system can prove its own consistency, you must first define a formal system precisely enough to reason about one, and the definitions Hilbert's school produced for that purpose are proof theory. The subject exists because he wanted something from it that it turned out not to contain. The question stopped being whether everything can be proved and became which things can be, by what means, at what cost, and every subsequent development in the field is an answer to a question in that form.

# Gödel

*Expertise has inherent limits.*

At six he had rheumatic fever and recovered completely. He read about the disease afterward, concluded that his heart had been permanently damaged, and held that conviction for the next sixty-five years against the unanimous opinion of every physician who examined him. The pattern is the whole man in miniature: a chain of reasoning from premises to a conclusion, followed with total fidelity, and unavailable to correction from outside.

Kurt Gödel was born in 1906 in Brünn, in what was then Austria-Hungary, to a textile factory manager and a mother of some cultivation. He went to Vienna for physics, moved to mathematics, and settled in logic. He attended the Vienna Circle and was never of it. The Circle held that a meaningful statement is either empirically checkable or true by convention; Gödel was a Platonist who held that mathematical objects exist independently of anyone's thinking about them and that mathematicians find rather than build. He kept this largely to himself for years, in rooms full of people whose central commitment it contradicted.

His 1930 dissertation proved the completeness of first-order predicate logic: every valid formula is provable by the standard rules. This was excellent news for Hilbert. It showed that for the logic underneath everything, the fit between truth and proof is exact.

The following year he showed that the fit fails as soon as the system can talk about arithmetic.

The paper is titled "On Formally Undecidable Propositions of *Principia Mathematica* and Related Systems," which is an accurate description and gives no indication of what is inside. The first theorem: any consistent formal system capable of expressing elementary arithmetic contains statements that are true and unprovable within it. Adding one as an axiom does not help, because the enlarged system generates its own. The second theorem: no such system proves its own consistency. If it does, it is inconsistent, and it will of course prove anything at all.

The mechanism is what makes it a theorem rather than a paradox. Gödel assigned numbers to symbols, then to formulas, then to sequences of formulas, so that the syntactic property of being a proof became an arithmetical property of a number. Arithmetic can then talk about arithmetic. Within that encoding he constructed a formula asserting its own unprovability. If it is provable the system proves a falsehood. If it is not provable, then it is true, and the system has a true statement it cannot reach. Nothing in the construction is circular, which is the part that took the work.

The self-reference is close enough to the liar paradox that the difference is the entire achievement. The liar is a contradiction and stops the discussion. Gödel's sentence is a theorem about the system, proved inside the system's own methods, with no contradiction anywhere. He turned the oldest known way of breaking a formal system into a way of measuring one.

Von Neumann was in the room at Königsberg in 1930 when Gödel first mentioned the result in discussion, understood it immediately, worked out the second theorem independently within weeks, and wrote to Gödel about it, at which point he learned Gödel already had it. Others took longer or never arrived. Zermelo rejected it outright. The philosophical readings that followed have ranged from careful to absurd and the absurd ones have had the wider circulation.

What the theorems do not say is worth stating, because most of the popular uses of them require the opposite. They do not show that mathematics is unreliable, that formal methods are futile, that human minds exceed machines, or that truth is out of reach. They establish that no single formal system captures all arithmetical truth, and that consistency must be established, if at all, from outside. That is a constraint on how formalization works. It is not an argument against doing it, and it was proved by exactly the methods it constrains.

He stayed in Vienna through the 1930s as a Privatdozent, an unsalaried position dependent on student fees. Austria was annexed in 1938. Gödel was not Jewish, but his associations were, and the new administration abolished his position, reviewed him, and classified him fit for military service. He was assaulted on the street by a gang who took him for a Jew; his wife Adele drove them off with her umbrella. In January 1940 the two of them left, east through the Soviet Union on the Trans-Siberian, by ship from Japan to San Francisco, and across the country by train to Princeton, taking the long way around a war that was closing behind them.

At the Institute for Advanced Study he became close to Einstein, who said he went to the Institute chiefly for the privilege of walking home with Gödel. Gödel proved the consistency of the axiom of choice and the continuum hypothesis with the rest of set theory, establishing that neither can be disproved. For Einstein's seventieth birthday he produced a solution to the field equations of general relativity in which closed timelike curves exist, so that a sufficiently determined traveler can reach his own past. He appears to have taken this as bearing on the reality of time rather than on the plausibility of the solution.

He also prepared for his American citizenship hearing by studying the Constitution and finding in it an internal inconsistency permitting the lawful installation of a dictatorship. Einstein and Morgenstern accompanied him to the hearing with instructions to say nothing about it. The judge asked whether such a thing could happen in America. Gödel began to explain how.

The paranoia deepened over the decades and settled on food. He believed he was being poisoned and would eat only what Adele prepared. In 1977 she was hospitalized for six months. He stopped eating. He died at Princeton Hospital on 14 January 1978 weighing sixty-five pounds, and the death certificate gives the cause as malnutrition and inanition brought on by personality disturbance.

For the project of formalization the consequence is precise and it is not discouraging. Incompleteness means no formalization is final; there are always truths outside the current system, and there is no last system. It does not follow that formalizing is pointless, only that it does not terminate. Each formalization captures something and none captures everything, which makes the correct response to the theorems the continuation of the work at a known and permanent discount.

# Turing

*Expertise cannot answer all questions.*

Turing was elected to a fellowship at King's in 1935, at twenty-two, on the strength of a dissertation proving the central limit theorem. Lindeberg had proved it in 1922. Turing had not known, had derived it himself, and the fellowship stood, which tells you both that the work was good and that he was not in the habit of checking whether a problem was already solved before solving it.

Alan Mathison Turing was born in London in 1912, to a father in the Indian Civil Service, and spent much of his childhood parked with foster families in England while his parents were abroad. School reports praise his mathematics and complain about everything else, chiefly his handwriting and his refusal to work on the assigned material. He went from Sherborne to King's College, Cambridge, and stayed.

The 1936 paper is called "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem," and the application in the title is the demolition of one of Hilbert's questions. The *Entscheidungsproblem* asks for a procedure that decides, for any statement of first-order logic, whether it is provable. Hilbert expected such a procedure to exist. To show that none does, Turing had first to say what a procedure is, and there was no definition. The word algorithm had been in use for a thousand years without anyone needing to pin it down, because nobody had previously wanted to prove that something was not one.

His definition is a machine so simple that its simplicity is the argument. An unbounded tape divided into cells, each holding one symbol from a finite alphabet. A head that reads one cell, writes one symbol, and moves one cell left or right. A finite set of internal states. A table specifying, for each state and symbol, what to write, which way to move, and which state to enter next. That is the entire apparatus, and Turing's claim was that anything a human clerk could do by following fixed instructions without insight, this machine can do. The claim cannot be proved, since one side of it is informal, and it has survived every attempt to find a counterexample and every competing formalization, all of which turned out to define the same class of functions.

Church had reached the same conclusion at Princeton by a different route, the lambda calculus, and published slightly earlier. Turing acknowledged the priority and the two definitions were shown equivalent. What Turing had that Church did not was a picture. The lambda calculus is a syntax; the machine is a device, and one can watch it work, which is why the thesis is generally called Church-Turing and the object is called a Turing machine.

The halting problem followed. There is no machine that takes a description of a machine and an input and decides whether that machine eventually stops. Assume one exists and you can build from it a machine that halts exactly when it does not. Since a decision procedure for provability in first-order logic would let you settle halting, no such procedure exists. Hilbert's tenth question and his decision problem were both answered no by machinery invented for the purpose.

The same paper contains the universal machine: a single machine that reads a description of any other machine from its tape and does what that machine would do. The distinction between hardware and program is fully present in it, nine years before anyone built a stored-program computer, and it arrived as a lemma in a proof about the limits of logic.

He spent 1936 to 1938 at Princeton with Church, took a doctorate, declined von Neumann's offer of an assistantship, and went home. In September 1939 he reported to Bletchley Park.

The German services encrypted with the Enigma, whose rotor settings changed daily and whose key space was too large to search. The break came from procedural regularities rather than from mathematics alone: predictable message openings, weather reports of known content, and the machine's guarantee that no letter ever encrypts to itself. Turing designed the Bombe around a mechanism that assumed a crib, propagated the logical consequences through a bank of rotors, and rejected settings that produced a contradiction, which is to say he built a machine that searched by refutation. The resulting intelligence, Ultra, mattered to the Atlantic convoys in a way that historians argue about only in degree. He also worked on the Lorenz cipher used for high command traffic, the attack on which produced Colossus, among the first electronic digital computers, though that machine was Flowers's design rather than his.

After the war he wrote a full design for a stored-program computer, the ACE, at the National Physical Laboratory. It was more ambitious than what the Americans were building and it was not built, at least not as specified, because the institution moved slowly and he did not. He went to Manchester, programmed the machine being built there, and wrote its manual.

In 1950 he published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," proposing that the question whether machines can think be replaced with a question that can be settled: whether an interrogator communicating by text can distinguish the machine from a person. The paper anticipates and answers nine objections, including the theological one, and is still the reference point for a debate it was written to make tractable.

In 1952 he turned to morphogenesis and asked how an initially uniform ball of cells produces stripes, spots, and structures. His answer was that two chemicals diffusing at different rates while reacting can amplify tiny fluctuations into stable patterns, and that the form of an animal can therefore be the solution to a differential equation rather than a plan. The paper founded a field and he had time for exactly one.

That year he reported a burglary to the police, the investigation established that he had been in a relationship with a man, and he was prosecuted for gross indecency. He did not contest the facts and appears to have regarded the law as absurd rather than dangerous. The alternative to prison was a course of synthetic oestrogen, which he took for a year. It caused gynecomastia and other effects. His security clearance was withdrawn, which ended the government work.

He was found dead at home on 8 June 1954, of cyanide poisoning. A partly eaten apple lay beside the bed and was never tested. The inquest returned suicide. He had been electroplating spoons with potassium cyanide in his spare room, he had made plans for the following week, and his mother maintained to the end that it was carelessness. He was forty-one.

An official apology came in 2009 and a royal pardon in 2013, both of which were offered to the public rather than to him.

The machine remains the definition of computation. The halting problem remains the standard undecidable one. The thesis remains the account of what algorithms can do, and the universal machine remains the reason a general-purpose computer is possible. All of it came out of an attempt to prove that a certain thing could not be done, which required inventing a precise account of what doing consists of, and the account has been worth incalculably more than the impossibility result it was built to establish.
# Meehl

*Experts are outperformed by formulas.*

Everything to this point concerns mathematics, where the case for making reasoning explicit is not seriously contested, because mathematics is the subject that consists of explicit reasoning. Whether any of it bears on medicine, law, or the ordinary business of deciding what to do about a particular person is a separate question, and it is not a question logic can answer. It is empirical. Someone had to check.

Paul Everett Meehl was born in Minneapolis in 1920, took his bachelor's degree at the University of Minnesota in 1941 and his doctorate there in 1945, joined the faculty, and stayed until he retired, holding appointments at various points in psychology, psychiatry, neurology, philosophy, and law. He died in Minneapolis in 2003. He was trained as a clinical psychologist, which meant he was trained to do the thing he spent his career demonstrating could not be done well.

The clinical task is prediction. Will this patient relapse, will this prisoner reoffend, will this applicant succeed, will this treatment help. The clinician interviews, tests, reads the file, and integrates. Integration is the word used for the part nobody can describe, and it was understood to be where the training paid for itself.

In 1954 Meehl published a short book comparing that method against the alternative, which is to take a few variables, weight them by fixed coefficients, add them up, and read off the answer. He reviewed roughly twenty studies across academic performance, parole outcomes, psychiatric diagnosis, job performance, and treatment response. In nearly every one, the equation matched or beat the expert. The equations were not sophisticated. Several were the kind of thing a competent undergraduate could fit in an afternoon.

The book is temperate, closely argued, and was received as an attack, which in fairness it was. Clinicians raised the objections one would expect. Individual cases are complex and no formula captures them. The expert sees things that do not fit in variables. Meehl took the objections seriously enough to test them, which is what makes the work durable, and they did not survive contact.

The complexity objection fails on the data. In studies where the clinician received everything the formula received plus the interview, the file, and the test battery, the clinician still lost. More information made the clinician worse, not better, because the additional material was mostly noise with a high subjective salience.

The nuance objection is subtler and Meehl gave it a name. A formula predicting whether a professor will attend the cinema tonight, fitted on his weekly habit, will fail if he has just broken his leg. The formula does not know about legs. A human does, and should override. Meehl granted this entirely, and then pointed out the difficulty: broken-leg cases are real and rare, clinicians cannot identify them reliably, and clinicians override constantly. Permitting the override to preserve the rare correct one imports all the incorrect ones, and the exchange is heavily unfavorable. The exception is genuine and using it costs more than it returns.

What remained was to explain why the equation wins, and the explanation is not that the equation is clever. The equation is stupid and that is the mechanism. It weights the same variables the same way every time. It does not sleep badly, does not like the patient, does not remember the last case that went wrong, does not notice that the file photograph resembles a nephew. Given identical inputs on two occasions it produces identical outputs, which is a property no clinician has: presented the same case twice under a disguise, the same expert regularly reaches different conclusions. The clinician is running a rough equation with noise added and no record of the coefficients. Removing the noise is the entire improvement, and it is enough.

The literature grew for fifty years and the finding held. By the Grove and Meehl meta-analysis of 1996, covering 136 studies, the mechanical method won or tied in the overwhelming majority, and the cases where the clinician won were scattered and did not form a pattern anyone could use in advance.

Meehl drew a boundary rather than a conclusion about human uselessness. Prediction should be mechanical wherever data exist to build and validate a formula. Everything a formula cannot do, which includes generating hypotheses, designing an intervention, deciding what is worth measuring, and sitting in a room with a frightened person, remains the clinician's. The division is clean and almost nobody adopted it.

He spent his later decades on the methodology of his field and grew steadily more scathing. He attacked the ritual of null hypothesis significance testing, pointing out that in social science the null is essentially never exactly true, so rejecting it at any threshold establishes almost nothing. He named the crud factor: in a large enough sample of human data, everything correlates with everything, so a significant correlation is evidence that you collected enough subjects. He observed that psychological theories are not refuted so much as abandoned, and that they suffer the fate of Douglas MacArthur's old soldiers, which is to fade away. He argued that the discipline mistook accumulation for progress because it never subjected its theories to tests that could have gone badly.

This was the 1960s, the 1970s, and the 1980s. The replication crisis arrived around 2011 and produced a large literature restating his arguments as discoveries.

His position here is not that of a logician. He built no systems and proved no theorems, and he supplied the one thing the logicians could not, which is evidence. Frege through Gödel established that reasoning can be made explicit; none of it establishes that anyone outside mathematics should bother. Meehl showed that in the domains where being wrong costs the most, the explicit rule beats the trained judgment, and it does so not in the laboratory but on parole boards and in admissions offices and in psychiatric wards.

The resistance was never really about the data, which is why fifty years of data did not move it. Accepting the finding requires accepting that years of training conferred something other than what it appeared to confer, and that the felt experience of expert insight is not tracking the thing it feels like it is tracking. That is an expensive thing to accept about yourself, and almost everyone declined.

# Millikan

*Expert authority corrupts measurement.*

The 1913 paper reporting the charge of the electron contains a sentence about method: "It is to be remarked, too, that this is not a selected group of drops but represents all of the drops experimented upon during 60 consecutive days." The laboratory notebooks are held at Caltech, they cover the period, and they record considerably more drops than the paper reports. Some carry annotations in Millikan's hand. One reads "Beautiful, publish this." Another reads "Very low, something wrong."

Robert Andrews Millikan was born in Illinois in 1868, the son of a Congregational minister, and grew up in Iowa. At Oberlin he was asked to teach the elementary physics course on the reasoning that a student who had done well in Greek could handle anything, and he took the job knowing no physics. He took his doctorate at Columbia in 1895, where he was the only physics graduate student in the department, spent a year in Germany, and joined Michelson at Chicago in 1896. He stayed twenty-five years, moved to the young California Institute of Technology in 1921, and ran it for the next quarter century, turning a small local college into a research institution by force of fundraising and recruitment. He received the Nobel Prize in 1923. He died in San Marino in 1953.

The oil drop experiment ran from 1909 to 1913 and settled a real question. Atomicity of charge was not established; there was a serious position holding that electrical charge was a continuous fluid and that the apparent electron was a statistical artifact. Millikan sprayed fine oil droplets into a chamber, charged them, and balanced them between plates, measuring the rate of fall under gravity against the rate of rise under the field. The charges came out as integer multiples of a single quantity. That result is correct, it is important, and it is his.

The number attached to it was wrong. He used a value for the viscosity of air that was slightly too low, and the resulting figure for the elementary charge sits about six tenths of a percent below the modern value. This is an ordinary scientific error of the sort that gets corrected, and the interest lies entirely in how long the correction took and why.

The graduate student who did much of the work was Harvey Fletcher. The two had an understanding: Fletcher would take sole authorship of one paper to serve as his dissertation, and Millikan would take sole authorship of the oil drop papers. Fletcher kept to it and went on to a distinguished career in acoustics at Bell Labs. He wrote his account of the arrangement to be published after his death, and it appeared in 1982.

Felix Ehrenhaft in Vienna ran comparable experiments and reported something different: charges smaller than Millikan's quantum, distributed continuously, which he called subelectrons. The dispute ran for more than a decade and was conducted with some heat. Millikan won it.

The uncomfortable part is that Millikan was right and Ehrenhaft was wrong. Ehrenhaft worked with smaller particles where Brownian motion and evaporation swamp the measurement, and his subelectrons were artifacts. This is not a case of a suppressed heretic. It is a case of a correct conclusion reached by a contest of authority and selective presentation, in which the man with the better apparatus and the larger reputation also declined to publish his full data and stated in print that he had. The method was defective and the answer was right, and a defective method that produces the right answer teaches nothing about how to tell.

The notebooks became public when Gerald Holton examined them in the 1970s, and the argument about what they show has continued since. Allan Franklin reanalyzed the excluded drops and concluded that including them would have shifted the published value very little, which is a real defence of the number and no defence at all of the sentence. Millikan appears to have discarded drops he judged compromised by apparatus trouble, which is a legitimate thing for an experimentalist to do and an illegitimate thing to conceal. What went into the literature was a claim of completeness that was not true, and it went in because it made the result harder to argue with.

There is a second episode that shows the same disposition from the other direction. Millikan spent the better part of a decade attempting to disprove Einstein's photon hypothesis, which he considered physically absurd. His experiments confirmed the photoelectric equation to high precision. He published the confirmation and continued to reject the theory, writing in 1916 that the hypothesis might well be called reckless and that his own work had verified the equation despite what he took to be the unreasonableness of what lay behind it. The Nobel citation covers both the charge measurement and the photoelectric work. He received it in part for establishing something he did not believe and had set out to destroy.

What makes the case belong here is what happened afterward, to other people's data.

Feynman put it in a Caltech commencement address in 1974. Plot the published measurements of the electron charge against time after Millikan and they do not jump to the correct value. They creep. Each successive determination sits a little above the last, and the sequence walks slowly upward over years until it arrives where it should have been at the start. His explanation was that when an experimenter got a number well above Millikan's he concluded something was wrong with his apparatus and went looking for the fault, and found one, because there is always something; and when he got a number close to Millikan's he did not look as hard. Nobody falsified anything. The asymmetry lived entirely in how long a person searched for an error before deciding he did not have one, and that duration was set by whose number he was disagreeing with.

The detailed record is less tidy than Feynman's telling, and historians have said so. The effect he described is nonetheless real and is no longer anecdotal. The Particle Data Group publishes historical plots of measured constants, and the pattern recurs across many quantities: values cluster near the accepted figure, then migrate as a group when the accepted figure moves, with error bars that do not honestly cover the distance travelled. The bias Feynman noticed by eye has since been measured.

This is a different failure from the two around it. Meehl's clinicians were individually unreliable and would have been unreliable in an empty room. Randi's physicists were individually deceived by a person working to deceive them. Millikan's effect requires no incompetence in any individual and no deception by anyone. Every experimenter in the sequence behaves reasonably. Each one, on getting a discrepant result, does exactly what careful practice demands, which is to suspect his own apparatus first. The distortion is produced by the aggregate of that reasonable behaviour in the presence of a published value carrying a name, and it is invisible from inside any single laboratory, because from inside a single laboratory it looks like diligence.

An authoritative wrong number is therefore self-protecting for as long as its authority lasts. It sets the threshold of scrutiny that every subsequent measurement must clear, and it sets that threshold asymmetrically, low for agreement and high for dissent. The literature does not record the truth converging on itself. It records a negotiation between what was found and what was expected, conducted by people who would each deny they were negotiating.

Millikan's name came off the Caltech building in 2021, for his service on the board of the Human Betterment Foundation, which promoted compulsory sterilization in California and supplied a model that was studied in Germany. That is a separate matter from the physics and it is not the reason he is here. He is here because a first-rate experimentalist, doing genuine work that reached a correct and important conclusion, published a false statement about his own procedure and thereby bent the measurement record of a fundamental constant for a generation, and none of the people who did the bending were doing anything they would have recognized as wrong.

# Randi

*Experts are easily deceived.*

Randi put it in one sentence, and it inverts the entire expected hierarchy: "I'd rather have an audience full of Ph.D.s than an audience full of children. Scientists are easier to deceive because they're so well educated. They're educated in a universe that behaves itself, not a universe that lies to them."

Randall James Hamilton Zwinge was born in Toronto in 1928, ran away with a carnival at seventeen, and worked for four decades as a stage magician and escape artist under the name The Amazing Randi, which included getting out of a sealed coffin underwater and out of a straitjacket suspended over Niagara Falls. He was good at it. The performance career is not the reason he is here; it is the qualification.

A physicist studies a system that does not adapt. Water does not notice being measured and change what it does. When an unexpected result appears, the physicist's trained response is to look for an artifact, a systematic error, an uncontrolled variable, and then, if those are excluded, a discovery. There is no step in that procedure for the possibility that something in the room is lying on purpose, because nothing in physics ever has. A magician's entire craft is the exploitation of that missing step. The expertise does not fail despite the training. It fails along a channel the training does not know exists.

In the early 1970s Uri Geller, an Israeli performer, bent cutlery and reproduced sealed drawings and stopped watches, and was tested at the Stanford Research Institute by Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ, both physicists. They published in *Nature* in 1974. The journal ran it with an unusual editorial note conceding that the referees had found the work weak and publishing it anyway on the grounds that the subject was better aired than suppressed. Targ and Puthoff were sufficiently persuaded to speak of a Geller effect.

The protocols were, to a magician, transparent. They were built to exclude sensory leakage, statistical artifact, and unconscious cueing, all of which are the failure modes of an honest subject. Geller was not an honest subject. He was a competent performer working against controls designed for someone else, and he handled the props before testing, and he was permitted latitude about conditions, and the results were remarkable.

Johnny Carson had worked as a magician before he was a broadcaster, and when Geller was booked on *The Tonight Show* in 1973 Carson called Randi. Randi's advice amounted to a control condition: the show would supply its own spoons and its own sealed containers, and Geller and his staff would not come near them beforehand. On the night, Geller sat with the props for twenty-two minutes and could do nothing at all, and said, several times, that he did not feel strong. The tape is the cleanest experiment ever run on the question and it ran on network television between a monologue and a musical guest.

In 1979 the McDonnell Laboratory for Psychical Research opened at Washington University in St. Louis on a $500,000 grant from the aviation executive James S. McDonnell, directed by Peter Phillips, a physics professor. Randi wrote to Phillips offering a set of protocols. They were not elaborate: have a magician present during testing, control the props, do not let the subject determine the conditions, film continuously. Phillips did not adopt them.

Randi then sent the laboratory two teenage magicians, Steve Shaw and Michael Edwards, presenting as gifted psychics. They bent cutlery below the table line. They swapped labels on sealed containers. They exploited every gap the protocols left, and the protocols left many, and over four years the laboratory came to regard them as its most promising subjects. Randi had given the two of them one standing instruction, which was that if a researcher ever asked them directly whether they were using conjuring methods, they were to say yes immediately. In four years nobody asked.

When rumours of a connection to Randi reached Phillips, he tightened the controls, and the abilities declined at once. Randi announced the hoax in January 1983 and called it Project Alpha. The laboratory had published no positive findings, which is the one thing that went right, and it closed in 1985.

The lesson is not that psychic ability is fictional, a claim that was already in evidence and that no demonstration was going to settle for the people who held the opposing view. The lesson is that a laboratory of credentialed scientists, running experiments in good faith with real funding and institutional oversight, was comprehensively deceived for four years by two adolescents with no training beyond stage magic, and that the deception was possible because the methodology was built against error and the problem was not error.

Randi founded his educational foundation in 1996 and offered a prize, eventually a million dollars, for a demonstration of any paranormal ability under conditions agreed in advance with the claimant. More than a thousand people applied. None passed a preliminary test. Randi read this as evidence about the abilities; believers read it as evidence about the protocols; the money was never paid and the foundation withdrew the offer in 2015. He died in Florida in 2020, aged ninety-two.

His bearing on formalization is oblique and it is not small. The logicians built their methods against honest error, because the mathematician who publishes a flawed proof is mistaken rather than lying, and the whole apparatus of checking assumes a cooperative author. Meehl showed that experts doing their best are outperformed. Randi showed that experts doing their best can be worked, deliberately, by someone who has studied how they check things, and that the credential is the attack surface rather than the defence.

The case for mechanizing verification does not require assuming that anyone is dishonest. It requires only noticing that human checking has failure modes its owners cannot see from the inside, and that a proof which can be verified by a procedure does not depend on the alertness, the good faith, or the incorruptibility of whoever is holding it. The physicists at Stanford and the researchers in St. Louis were careful people. Care was not the relevant variable.
# Curry and Howard

*Expertise compiles.*

The deepest structural fact in this subject was first noticed as a passing remark in a book about something else, and then rediscovered in a manuscript that circulated in photocopy for eleven years because its author saw no reason to hurry. Neither man appears to have thought he had found anything urgent.

Haskell Brooks Curry was born in Massachusetts in 1900 to a father who taught elocution and a mother who had read mathematics at Oberlin. He took his degree at Harvard, worked briefly for General Electric, returned to graduate study, and finished his doctorate at Göttingen in 1930 under Hilbert, with Bernays doing most of the actual supervision. He spent his career at Penn State. He died in 1982. William Alvin Howard was born in 1926, took his doctorate at Chicago in 1956 under Saunders Mac Lane, and spent his career at the University of Illinois at Chicago.

Curry's subject was combinatory logic, which he developed as a contribution to the Hilbert program: a system of logic with no bound variables at all. Where Church's lambda calculus writes the identity function as λx.x, combinatory logic assembles everything from a few primitive combinators. K takes two arguments and returns the first. S is a distribution operator whose definition is less memorable than its consequence, which is that S and K together suffice to express every computable function. Variables, and all the machinery of substitution and renaming they drag behind them, turn out to be a convenience rather than a necessity.

Assigning types to these combinators, Curry noticed that the types were familiar. K has the type A → (B → A), which is to say: A implies that B implies A. That is an axiom of propositional logic. S has a type that is likewise an axiom schema. The rule for applying one typed combinator to another has the same form as modus ponens. He recorded the observation, noted that the type structure of combinators mirrors the axiom structure of implicational logic, and moved on. It appears in his 1958 book with Robert Feys as a structural curiosity.

Howard picked it up in 1969 and worked out that the curiosity was total. He wrote it down, and the manuscript passed hand to hand among people who worked on type theory until it was finally published in 1980, in a volume assembled to mark Curry's eightieth birthday. The two halves of the correspondence met in print in a festschrift.

What Howard showed is that Gentzen's natural deduction proofs and the terms of the typed lambda calculus are the same objects. Not analogous. The same. Every logical connective is a type constructor. A proof of A → B is a function taking proofs of A to proofs of B. A proof of A ∧ B is a pair. A proof of A ∨ B is a tagged value indicating which side you have. Universal quantification is a dependent function type and existential quantification is a dependent pair. Every rule of inference is a programming construct: modus ponens is function application, and discharging an assumption to conclude an implication is lambda abstraction. Under the correspondence, checking that a program has the type it claims is checking that a proof proves what it claims. A type error is a logical error. The compiler was doing proof theory the whole time without being told.

The correspondence holds for intuitionistic logic rather than classical, and the reason is instructive rather than a limitation to be apologized for. Intuitionistic logic refuses the law of excluded middle, and under the correspondence the refusal has a mechanical cause. A proof of ∃x.P(x) is a pair consisting of a witness and a proof that the witness works. If you cannot produce the witness you have not produced the pair, and you have no proof. Classical existence proofs that establish something must exist without indicating what it is have no corresponding program, because there is nothing for the program to return. The philosophical dispute about constructive mathematics, which had been conducted for decades in the vocabulary of intuition and meaning, turns out to be a dispute about whether your proofs run.

Proof simplification and program evaluation are likewise the same operation. A proof that introduces a lemma and immediately applies it corresponds to a function definition followed by a call, and evaluating the call by substituting the argument is exactly Gentzen's cut elimination performed on the proof. A proof in normal form is a fully evaluated program.

The practical consequences arrive without any further theory. Proofs can be represented as data structures, because they are terms. Proofs can be verified by running a type checker, because verification is type checking. The considerable engineering effort that had gone into compilers, type inference, and polymorphism was, without anyone having planned it, an investment in theorem proving. Type inference becomes proof search. Polymorphism becomes proof generalization.

The correspondence also joined two projects that had been pursued separately by people who mostly did not read each other. The logicists from Frege forward wanted to reduce mathematics to logic. The computability theorists from Turing forward wanted to characterize mechanical calculation. Curry and Howard established that these are one subject examined from two directions, and that a result in either is a result in both.

None of this was technology. It was a theorem stating that proofs and programs are the same mathematical object, and a theorem does not check anything. Type checking was proof checking in principle, and whether it could be proof checking in practice depended on questions no theorem addresses: whether a system could be built, whether anyone would write proofs in a form it would accept, and whether the verification would be trustworthy enough to be worth the trouble. The foundation was finished. Nobody had laid a brick.

The first brick was laid in the Netherlands in 1967, by a combinatorialist approaching fifty who had concluded that mathematicians could not be relied upon to check each other's work.

# de Bruijn

*Expertise can be checked by machine.*

Nicolaas Govert de Bruijn was born in The Hague in 1918, published his first paper at seventeen while still at school, and took his doctorate at Leiden in 1943 on the asymptotic distribution of primes. He moved to Eindhoven in 1960 and stayed. He worked in number theory, combinatorics, analysis, and geometry, and the de Bruijn sequences are his: cyclic strings in which every substring of a given length occurs exactly once, which turn out to be useful for anything that needs to identify position from a local reading. He did substantial work on asymptotic methods, on Pólya enumeration, and later on quasicrystals and aperiodic tilings. He died in 2012 at ninety-three, and any one of those lines would have been a career.

What he is here for began around 1967, when he was forty-nine, and it began with an observation about a profession rather than a problem in mathematics.

Mathematicians write proofs in prose with symbols in it. The proofs are checked by readers, and the readers bring attention, training, and a finite tolerance for tedium. The system works in the aggregate: mathematics advances, and errors are eventually noticed. It is also unreliable in ways the profession preferred not to examine closely. Referees skim. Gaps that one reader fills silently from context are gaps another reader would have caught. A proof accepted by one expert is rejected by another, and the disagreement is settled socially. The reliability of the whole edifice rests on the alertness of people reading long documents carefully, without pay, in their spare time.

De Bruijn proposed to remove them from the critical path. Not from finding proofs, which he took to require whatever it is that finding proofs requires. From checking them, which requires only the systematic application of fixed rules and is therefore precisely the sort of work that people do badly and machines do perfectly.

He called the system Automath, began in 1967, and described it in 1968. It does not search for proofs and it will not help you find one. It reads a proof you have written and tells you whether it is valid. The human retains the creative work in full; the machine takes the tedium, which is where the errors live.

The design decisions have all survived. Automath was built on a type theory in which propositions are types and proofs are terms, which is the Curry-Howard correspondence turned into an implementation rather than a remark. To check a proof, check that the term has the type it claims. The check is mechanical because typing rules are mechanical.

Second, and more consequential, de Bruijn insisted that the correctness of the whole system should depend on a small kernel that a person can read in an afternoon. The kernel implements the typing rules and nothing else. Everything above it, the interface, the libraries, the conveniences, the automation, can be large, complicated, and buggy, and a bug there can waste your time but cannot produce a false theorem, because everything must pass the kernel to count. Trust is not distributed across the system. It is concentrated in one small place, deliberately, so that it can be audited.

That is the de Bruijn criterion, and it is the only durable answer anyone has produced to the question of why you should believe a machine. Milner's LCF approach in the 1970s, in which theorems are an abstract type constructible only by applying inference rules, is the same idea arrived at again. Every serious proof assistant since has been built on it.

The project ran about fifteen years. Its largest product was the formalization of Landau's *Grundlagen der Analysis*, the standard construction of the real numbers, completed by de Bruijn's student van Benthem Jutting in 1977. The exercise turned up a small number of genuine errors in a textbook that had been in print and in use for half a century, which was reassuring about Landau and not reassuring about the reading habits of everyone who had used him.

Automath was not adopted. The reasons were practical and they were severe. Writing a proof in it took vastly longer than writing one on paper, the hardware of the period made every iteration slow, and the interface was punishing. The mathematicians who might have wanted verified proofs were unwilling to pay in years for a benefit they experienced as theoretical. The system remained a research project, well known to about forty people.

Its ideas travelled anyway, mostly through those forty. Martin-Löf's type theory drew on it. The designers of Coq knew the work and built on it. De Bruijn indices, which represent a bound variable by counting how many binders outward you must go to find it, and thereby dispose of variable naming entirely, are standard in every implementation of type theory that exists.

De Bruijn's contribution was not the theory. Curry and Howard had supplied the theory and it was sitting there. What he added was the decision that it was an engineering specification rather than an observation, and then the engineering. He built the first machine that checked mathematics, and it worked, and its limitations were entirely about clock speed and patience.

Asked late in life what he was proudest of, with the sequences and the enumeration theory and the tilings all available to him, he said Automath. He did not see it become ordinary. He saw enough to know it would.

# Coquand and Huet

*Expertise can be kernel-checked.*

Thierry Coquand was born in 1961 in Jallieu, studied at the École Normale Supérieure, and took his doctorate at Paris VII in 1985 under Gérard Huet. He worked at INRIA and then moved to Gothenburg. Gérard Huet was born in 1947 in Bourges, took his doctorate at Case Western in 1972, and spent his career at INRIA working on automated theorem proving, type theory, and later computational linguistics. He was elected to the French Academy of Sciences in 2002.

The system they built is named Coq, which is French for rooster, is the national emblem, abbreviates the Calculus of Constructions, and is the first syllable of Coquand. French computer science does not often get to do this and it made the most of the opportunity.

The theory came first, as Coquand's thesis. The Calculus of Constructions welds Martin-Löf's dependent types, in which a type may be indexed by a value so that "vector of length n" is expressible, to Girard's System F, in which a single definition may be instantiated at many types. The combination is powerful enough to serve as a foundation for mathematics, and it is simultaneously a programming language, because under Curry-Howard those are the same thing. There is no boundary in the system between stating a theorem and declaring a type, or between proving one and writing a program.

The implementation followed from 1984 and has been under continuous development ever since, by a great many people at INRIA and elsewhere. Almost everything has changed in four decades. The three commitments have not: a kernel small enough to read, a type theory rich enough to express real mathematics, and the identification of proofs with programs.

The kernel does one job. It takes a term and a claimed type and decides whether the term has it. If it accepts, the theorem holds, subject to the kernel being correct, and that assumption is testable in the only way such assumptions ever are, by writing an independent implementation and seeing whether the two agree on a large corpus. Above the kernel sits everything that makes the system usable: tactics that discharge routine goals, libraries of existing mathematics, a language for stating specifications, and an interactive loop in which the user is shown what remains to be proved. The user works in that loop and generally never looks at the term being assembled underneath. The term is nonetheless what the theorem rests on, and the loop is a convenience for producing it.

What the system produced within two decades settled the question of whether any of this was practical.

Xavier Leroy and his collaborators built CompCert, a compiler for a large fragment of C, and proved that any program it compiles behaves as the source language semantics says it should. Not tested against a suite. Proved. A later study that threw randomized test programs at production compilers found substantial numbers of wrong-code bugs in GCC and LLVM and none in CompCert's verified core, which is the expected result and is nonetheless a strange thing to see confirmed empirically.

In 2009 a team at NICTA in Australia completed the verification of seL4, an operating system microkernel, establishing that the C implementation refines its specification and that the specification enforces its security properties. That work was done in Isabelle rather than Coq, and it belongs here anyway, because the question it answered was whether the approach scales to systems software written under real constraints.

Georges Gonthier formalized the four-color theorem in Coq in 2005. The history matters. The 1976 proof by Appel and Haken reduced the problem to a large case analysis and discharged the cases by computer, and mathematicians distrusted it for thirty years, on the grounds that no human could check the computation. Gonthier's response was to make the situation worse in a way that made it better. He did not verify the case analysis by hand; he reconstructed the entire argument, the mathematical reasoning as well as the computation, in a form no human will ever read, and had the kernel check all of it. The proof is now believed precisely because it has been removed further from human inspection. What changed is that the thing doing the inspecting is four thousand lines long and has been audited by everyone who cared to.

He then spent six years leading a team through the Feit-Thompson odd order theorem, whose original proof occupies more than two hundred pages of dense group theory, and finished in 2012 at roughly 150,000 lines of Coq. Formalizing it required building out substantial parts of finite group theory, character theory, and Galois theory as reusable libraries, which is the usual pattern: the theorem is the occasion and the infrastructure is the product.

Coquand went on to cubical type theory, which supplies computational content for Voevodsky's univalence axiom and thereby makes it something a machine can run rather than merely assume. Huet moved to computational linguistics and the formal analysis of Sanskrit. Coq acquired competitors in Lean, Agda, and Isabelle, and a community large enough that the field no longer depends on any particular person.

The costs are real and should be stated. Formalizing a proof takes something between ten and a hundred times as long as writing it informally. The learning curve is severe. Finished proofs are frequently unreadable, and a formalization that establishes a theorem may explain nothing about why it is true. These are engineering problems and they have been improving steadily, and they are the reason most mathematics is still not formalized.

What the system changed is the meaning of the claim. A theorem proved in Coq is not a consensus among people qualified to have one. It is a term, a type, and a program that accepted the pairing, and anyone may run that program themselves. The ambiguity of prose is gone, and so is the requirement to trust the author, the referee, or the journal. This is what Leibniz was describing and could not build, and what Hilbert wanted and Gödel bounded. The bounds hold: incompleteness means not everything true can be proved, and undecidability means not every proof can be found by search. Inside the bounds, a machine now decides whether a claimed proof is one, and it does not consult anybody.

# Voevodsky

*Expert verification is unreliable.*

In 1991 Vladimir Voevodsky and Mikhail Kapranov published a paper on ∞-groupoids. In 1998 Carlos Simpson published a paper containing a counterexample to its main theorem. Voevodsky read the counterexample, was not persuaded, and continued to believe his own result. He became convinced that Simpson was right in 2013, twenty-two years after publication and fifteen years after the refutation appeared in print.

For most of that period the mathematical community had before it a theorem and a published refutation of the theorem, and did not resolve which was correct, because doing so required a level of technical engagement that almost nobody had and the two people who had it disagreed. The author of the false result was among the strongest mathematicians alive and was, by his own later account, unable to see the error in his own work for a decade and a half.

Voevodsky was born in Moscow in 1966 to a physicist father and a chemist mother. He was expelled from Moscow State University for not attending lectures, which he considered a poor use of time, and continued working on his own. Papers he circulated reached David Kazhdan at Harvard, who arranged his admission to the graduate program without an application. He took his doctorate in 1992. His work on motivic homotopy theory imported the machinery of algebraic topology into algebraic geometry and produced, among other things, a proof of the Milnor conjecture. He received the Fields Medal in 2002 at thirty-six.

Then he changed subjects, and the reason he gave was error.

The Kapranov paper was the sharpest case but not the only one. He described finding gaps in other published work he had relied on, and a further error in his own. What he drew from it was not embarrassment but a conclusion about method: the informal system of proof and peer review does not scale to the complexity that mathematics had reached. Papers are long, dependencies are deep, the number of people qualified to check any given argument is small, and those people are busy with their own work. A wrong result can stand for fifteen years with a correct refutation sitting next to it in the literature. He could not trust the field and he could not trust himself, and neither could anyone else, and the profession's response to this was to continue as before.

His solution was formalization, adopted not as an interesting research direction but as a working necessity. If proofs are checked by machine, the errors humans miss are caught, and reliability stops depending on how carefully a referee read something on a train.

Working through the existing tools, he concluded that the available foundations were wrong for the mathematics he did. Set theory encodes everything as sets of sets, which forces arbitrary choices that then have to be carried around, and it makes isomorphic objects distinguishable in ways mathematicians consider irrelevant and spend energy ignoring. Homotopy type theory fits better: a type behaves like a space, a term like a point in it, and a proof of equality like a path between points. Iterating gives paths between paths, and the whole structure that algebraic topology was invented to study appears in the type theory for free.

To this he added the univalence axiom, which says that equivalent types are equal. In set theory, two isomorphic groups are different objects that happen to be indistinguishable by group-theoretic means, and every argument that transports a fact across the isomorphism has to do so by hand. Univalence makes the transport automatic by making the isomorphism an equality. It licenses formally the thing every mathematician already does informally, which is to treat isomorphic objects as the same object and stop worrying.

He spent his last decade on it, at the Institute for Advanced Study, in the building where Gödel had spent his. He organized the special year that produced the Homotopy Type Theory book in 2013, written by a large collaboration and formalized as it was written, so that the book about machine-checked mathematics was itself machine-checked. He also built libraries, wrote code, and did the unglamorous work, which a Fields Medalist is not required to do and which was the point.

The parallel with the two preceding chapters is exact and not accidental. Meehl showed that expert prediction is unreliable. Randi showed that expert observation is unreliable. Voevodsky showed that expert verification is unreliable, and the demonstration was that he had been one of the experts and it had happened to him.

The difference is what happened next. Meehl had fifty years of data and clinicians went on using judgment. Randi had a hoax that ran four years and parapsychology continued. Voevodsky had a tool, and he had the standing to make people look at it, and mathematicians began, slowly, to use it. Formalized mathematics remains a small fraction of the output. The fraction is growing, the major theorems are accumulating, and a generation is now learning proof assistants alongside the traditional apparatus.

He died in Princeton on 30 September 2017, of an aneurysm, at fifty-one. He left a wife and two children, to whom he was not a Fields Medalist.

The technical legacy is homotopy type theory and univalence. The other legacy is the example, and it changed the terms of the argument by changing who was making it. The case for formalization had been available for decades and had been made mostly by computer scientists, which allowed mathematicians to file it as somebody else's enthusiasm. When a Fields Medalist at the height of his powers concluded that his discipline's verification methods had failed and spent his remaining years building replacements, the filing became harder.

The errors he found were not anomalies and the fix is not more diligence. Human verification does not scale. Proofs lengthen, dependency chains deepen, the qualified readership for any particular argument shrinks, and the probability that a mistake survives review approaches certainty. The only remedy is to move the checking off the humans.

That is where the line running from Alexandria arrives. Euclid established what an explicit argument looks like. Frege and Russell showed one could be written in symbols. Gödel and Turing established what such systems cannot do. Curry and Howard identified proofs with programs. De Bruijn built the first checker and Coquand and Huet built the one people use. Voevodsky supplied the reason to bother. The case is not that formalization would be an improvement. The case is that the alternative has been measured and it does not work.

Necessity, however, compels nothing. Voevodsky's arguments are in print and free. The tools download in minutes and cost nothing. The errors in the literature are still there, discoverable by anyone who checks. Almost nobody checks. Some decline because the effort is real and the incentives point elsewhere, which is a defensible position for an individual and a disastrous one for a field. Some decline because their standing rests on the presumption that their judgment is sound, and a machine that checks proofs is an instrument for testing that presumption. And some reject not formalization but the entire apparatus of which it is one small component, holding that the machinery of technological civilization is the problem and that extending its reach into judgment itself is the problem intensified.

That last position is not a minority curiosity and it has been growing. The expansion of artificial intelligence since 2023 has been accompanied by a sharp revival of anti-technology ideology, and its central icon is a mathematician who mailed bombs to strangers for seventeen years. The manifesto circulates freely. He is referred to affectionately. The affection is not always ironic, and it is worth understanding what is being admired, because the capabilities that provoke it will continue to expand and the number of people who experience that expansion as a personal threat will expand with them.
# Kaczynski

*Experts may want you dead.*

Theodore John Kaczynski was born in Chicago in 1942, tested as gifted, and skipped two grades, arriving at Harvard at sixteen. There he was recruited into a psychological study run by Henry Murray in which subjects were subjected to sustained personal abuse under laboratory conditions, a fact frequently offered in his defence and which explains nothing, since the other subjects did not do what he did. He took his doctorate in mathematics at Michigan at twenty-five on boundary functions, work a member of his committee estimated that perhaps ten or twelve people in the country could appreciate, and won the department's prize for it. Berkeley appointed him assistant professor, the youngest in the university's history. He resigned after two years and gave no reason. He moved to a cabin in Montana without electricity or running water and lived in it for twenty-five years.

Between 1978 and 1995 he mailed or planted sixteen bombs. Three people were killed: Hugh Scrutton, who owned a computer rental store in Sacramento and picked up something left in his parking lot; Thomas Mosser, an advertising executive, in his kitchen in New Jersey with his family in the house; and Gilbert Murray, a timber industry lobbyist in California, who opened a package addressed to a predecessor. Twenty-three others were injured, several maimed. The FBI ran the investigation for seventeen years at a cost exceeding any in the agency's history.

In 1995 he offered to stop if a major newspaper would print his manuscript. The Washington Post published "Industrial Society and Its Future" in September, with the Times sharing the cost, on the advice of the Attorney General and the FBI director. It runs to 232 numbered paragraphs, adopts the register of analysis, and argues that industrial technology has damaged human psychological wellbeing, that the damage cannot be repaired within the existing arrangement, and that the arrangement should therefore be destroyed. His brother David recognized the phrasing and the argument and contacted the FBI. Kaczynski was arrested at the cabin in April 1996.

He wanted to conduct his own defence and argue that the killings were politically justified. His lawyers wanted to argue that he was mentally ill, which he refused to permit, and the competency proceedings that followed produced the leverage for a plea. He took life without parole to avoid a capital trial and thereby lost the platform, which he regarded as the greater loss. He died in federal custody in June 2023.

The credentials were real. The dissertation was genuine mathematics and the appointment was earned. He is not a man who failed to gain entry to the institutions he later attacked; he was admitted to the most selective of them at every stage, and left.

The Luddites broke machinery to defend a specific arrangement of skilled labour and wages, and their violence was aimed at property and calculated to produce a concession. Kaczynski's was not instrumental in that sense. He did not believe that killing an advertising executive would halt any development, and said as much: the bombings were to secure attention for the argument, and the argument was that the system should collapse. The people he killed were not obstacles to a goal. They were the price of publication.

The position beneath it is not original and has recurred in every generation since the first mills. Technology is not neutral, it reshapes human life in ways nobody chose and nobody can reverse, and the costs exceed the benefits. It is a recurring fantasy and it is checkable. Life expectancy at birth in the United States in 1900 was forty-seven years. In 2000 it was seventy-seven. The thirty years were not produced by wisdom or social arrangement. They came from vaccines, antibiotics, sanitation, refrigeration, and the capacity to move food and medicine faster than pathogens travel. Whoever holds that industrial civilization is a net loss is holding that those thirty years should be returned, and is generally not the person from whom they would be taken.

What he added to the grievance was the willingness to kill for it, and underneath the willingness was a specific conceit: the certainty that he alone was undeceived, that among several billion people he had seen the thing clearly and the rest were sleepwalking. He resolved that the tools should not exist and concluded that murdering people who built them was the proportionate response. The tools continued. The three men remained dead. Seventeen years of work produced a manuscript and a life sentence.

He belongs in this account because the category he occupies is real. Experts are outperformed by formulas, and experts are deceived by teenagers, and experts fail to verify their own results for fifteen years, and a trained mathematician may also decide that the people building the apparatus should be killed and act on it. That is not the typical response and it does not need to be typical to be a fact about the conditions under which this work proceeds.

The instruments of formal verification are themselves technology. They run on computers, they depend on compilers and languages, and they extend mechanical reasoning into territory previously reserved for human judgment, which makes them a case of the thing being objected to rather than an exception to it. Opposition of this kind does not arrive as a paper. It arrived, once, as a package.

He failed, and the impulse did not die with him. It circulates in the continued readership, in the affectionate nickname, in the half-ironic citation that is not entirely ironic, and in the part of an educated mind that entertains the thought that none of this should have been built. Anyone doing this work should expect that audience and should not expect to persuade it.

There is a second obstacle and it is nothing like the first. It does not want anyone dead. It wants only to decide what counts.

The apparatus that grew from Frege through Coq has produced verified compilers, verified kernels, verified cryptography, and formalized proofs of theorems that resisted human checking for decades. It has also settled into a narrow range. Practitioners are few, and the incentives that govern them are the ordinary academic ones, so effort concentrates where publications and grants are: compiler correctness, protocol soundness, foundations. Medical decision protocols, legal reasoning, institutional rules, the operational logic of the systems that actually govern people's days, remain untouched, not because they resist formalization but because formalizing them advances nobody's career.

The boundary between the problems worth attention and the problems beneath it is enforced by people with the standing to enforce it, and that standing has never been given an account of itself. The last time anyone demanded one, in terms sharp enough to be worth repeating, it was demanded by soldiers in a church in Putney who had just won a civil war and were arguing about what winning had entitled them to.

# Rainsborough

*Expert rule is usurpation.*

Thomas Rainsborough was baptized in Wapping in 1610. His father William was a naval commander who had taken a squadron to Salé to fight the Barbary corsairs and bring home English captives. The son went to sea first, then into the New Model Army, and rose to colonel of foot, serving at Bristol, at Berkeley Castle, and at Worcester. He was returned to Parliament for Droitwich in 1646. He was the most senior officer in the army to take the Leveller side, which meant he was the one man who could argue against Cromwell's son-in-law without being outranked into silence.

In the autumn of 1647 the war was won and the question was what the winning had been for. The General Council of the Army met in the church of St Mary the Virgin at Putney to settle it. The men in the room held the military power in England and were trying to work out what, if anything, that entitled them to. The proceedings survive because William Clarke, the assistant secretary, took them down in shorthand. His papers went to Worcester College and sat unread for two and a half centuries until C. H. Firth published them in the 1890s.

On 29 October the debate reached the franchise. Henry Ireton put the case for restriction, and he put it well. No person, he argued, has a right to a share in disposing of the affairs of the kingdom who has not a permanent fixed interest in this kingdom. Land, or a trade, or something that binds a man's fortune to the country's. The reasoning is not contemptible and it has never gone away: a man with nothing at stake bears no cost for a bad decision, and admitting him admits judgment that is free to be careless.

Rainsborough answered:

> For really I think that the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he; and therefore truly, sir, I think it's clear, that every man that is to live under a government ought first by his own consent to put himself under that government; and I do think that the poorest man in England is not at all bound in a strict sense to that government that he hath not had a voice to put himself under.

The sentence is usually read as a claim about the poor: that they are capable, that they deserve inclusion, that the franchise should be widened to take them in. That is not quite what he says, and the difference is the whole argument.

He does not say the poorest man is prudent, or learned, or fit to judge. He says he has a life to live. It is the plainest thing that can be said about a person and it is the thing Ireton's account has no room for, because a life is not an interest in the kingdom and cannot be weighed against acreage. Everything follows from it. A man who has a life to live is living it, and a government that disposes of it without his consent is disposing of something that was never handed over. The conclusion is stated in the negative: the poorest man is not bound. Rainsborough is not asking that a right be extended to him. He is observing that an obligation was never created.

He made the same point again in that exchange, and here nothing is asserted at all:

> I do not find anything in the Law of God, that a lord shall choose twenty burgesses, and a gentleman but two, or a poor man shall choose none: I find no such thing in the Law of Nature, nor in the Law of Nations.

He has looked for the law that gives the lord his twenty burgesses. He has looked in divine law, in natural law, and in the law of nations, which between them exhaust the sources a seventeenth-century Englishman could appeal to. It is not there. The lord holds his position, and men obey him, and no one has ever been able to say on what authority. The claim that requires a warrant is the claim to govern, and it is the one that has never had one.

Ireton heard this correctly, which is the best evidence for the reading. He did not reply by defending the competence of freeholders. He said that to deny the property qualification was to fly for refuge to an absolute natural right, and that an absolute natural right would dissolve property along with the franchise, since a man's title to his land rests on the same conventions as his title to a vote. He was not wrong. Cromwell, presiding, said the argument tended very much to anarchy. Neither of them thought they were being asked to widen a franchise.

The Levellers lost everything within eighteen months. Rainsborough was appointed vice-admiral in 1648 and the fleet refused to receive him and put him back ashore. He was sent north to the siege of Pontefract, and on 29 October 1648, a year to the day after he spoke at Putney, a Royalist party rode into Doncaster before dawn to take him alive and exchange him for Marmaduke Langdale. He fought them in the street outside his lodging and they killed him there. He was thirty-eight. His body was brought to London and buried at Wapping, where he had been baptized, and the funeral procession was enormous, and the mourners wore sea-green ribbons, which the movement took for its colour afterward.

In May 1649 the Leveller regiments mutinied and Cromwell caught them at Burford, held them overnight in the parish church, and in the morning shot three of them against the churchyard wall while the rest were made to watch from the roof: Cornet James Thompson, Corporal Perkins, Private John Church. That was the end of the Levellers as a movement. Manhood suffrage came to England in 1918.

The defeat was total and the argument was never answered. Nobody today makes Ireton's case in Ireton's terms. No one holds that a man's voice in his own governance should scale with his acreage, and the position is not so much refuted as unsayable. Ireton won every engagement and lost the proposition entirely, and the sentence Rainsborough was arguing when he made an enemy of the man who would rule England is now so ordinary that it is quoted at ceremonies.

The bearing on this work is narrow, and it is not an argument about who is clever enough to do formal mathematics. The referee, the programme committee, the funding panel, and the senior figure whose approval marks work as serious are all making Ireton's claim in its modern form: a permanent fixed interest in the field, held through training and position and publication, is what qualifies a person to say what counts. Rainsborough's question is available and has not been answered. There is no law of God, of nature, or of nations under which a committee determines whether a theorem holds. Whether a proof is valid is not a matter over which any person has ever held title, and the appearance that someone does has been sustained by custom and by there having been no alternative.

There is an alternative now, and its virtue is that it does not govern. A kernel has no interest in the outcome, no faction, no career, and no recollection of who submitted the term. A person who runs it has consented to a check and to nothing else, and the check returns what it returns. That is not authority of a better kind. It is the absence of the thing Rainsborough could not find a warrant for.

He contested the title from inside the polity that held it, standing in front of the men who held it, and it cost him his life and his movement. There is another way at it, which does not dispute the warrant at all.
# GG Allin

*Expert gatekeeping is optional.*

Kevin Michael Allin was born in Lancaster, New Hampshire in 1956 and named Jesus Christ Allin, because his father had been told in a vision that the boy would be a messiah. Merle Allin senior kept the family in a cabin without electricity or running water, dug graves in the basement, and talked about the coming end. The mother eventually left with the two boys. The name was changed to something usable when the child went to school. GG came from his older brother, who as a toddler could not manage Jesus.

He formed bands in New Hampshire and Vermont in the late 1970s, and the early records are ordinary punk, aggressive within the conventions of the form. He could sing. He wrote songs with structures in them. This is generally forgotten, and it matters, because the argument he later made would be worth nothing from someone who had no other option.

By the early 1980s the performances had left the conventions of any form. He appeared naked, defecated on stage and threw it, opened himself with broken glass, attacked members of the audience without warning, and invited them to attack him back. Sets typically ran fifteen or twenty minutes before the police arrived or the venue cut the power. He was arrested continually, jailed repeatedly, hospitalized often, and served a prison term in Michigan for assault. He came out and resumed.

He was explicit about why, in interviews and in the documentary Todd Phillips filmed shortly before his death. Rock music had been domesticated. The performer had become an entertainer, the audience expected to be safe, the venue expected compliance, the label expected a product. Punk had promised disruption and delivered a clothing style. His response was to make the performance genuinely dangerous, to collapse the distance between a show and an assault, so that nobody in the room could maintain the position of a spectator.

He called it the mission. The stage was a war zone. The audience was to be afraid. The performer was to be prepared to die.

The method is the part worth attention. He did not withdraw. He did not build a parallel scene or seek some uncontaminated space outside the industry. He played the clubs, used their stages and their microphones and their amplifiers, took the bookings from whoever would still have him, and released records through whatever label was interested. He used the apparatus completely and refused to use it as intended, and the apparatus could not stop him, because there was no credential to revoke, no licence to suspend, no permission that had been granted and could be taken back. Everything it controlled was something he had stopped wanting.

His brother Merle drummed for him across most of the incarnations. Others cycled through. The project could be staffed, which means it was a practice rather than a symptom.

He announced repeatedly that he would kill himself on stage on Halloween. The announcements sold tickets and he never did it. He died on 28 June 1993 of a heroin overdose at a party after a show in Manhattan, aged thirty-six. He was buried in an open casket, unwashed, in his leather jacket, with mourners dropping bottles and drugs into the coffin, and the photographs went around.

He never killed anyone. The violence was constant, extreme, and directed at himself as much as anyone else. He assaulted, he desecrated, and he destroyed himself, and he did not murder. That line separates him from the man in the preceding chapter, and it is not a technicality. Kaczynski's method foreclosed; Allin's opened. One of them ended other people's options and the other only ever risked his own.

The formal methods community has its own machinery of legitimation, and it works the way all such machinery works. Universities grant the positions. Journals and conferences confer the recognition. Funding bodies allocate the resources. Senior figures decide what is serious. The output has been substantial and none of it is in question here.

The constraint is what the machinery also does. Some problems are recognized as worth formalizing and some are not, and the boundary is maintained without anyone having to defend it. Papers on unfashionable applications are rejected from the venues that matter. Students who work on them do not get positions. Grant panels fund what grant panels fund. The result is that the operational logic of medicine, law, institutional procedure, and the ordinary rules that govern people's lives goes unformalized, not for any reason internal to the mathematics, but because the people who could do it are employed by institutions that reward other things.

The tools are not gated. Coq is open source. The Calculus of Inductive Constructions is documented in public. The techniques are in papers anyone can download. Nothing whatever prevents their application to any domain, except the belief that permission is required.

Allin's discovery was that the gates function only on people who want through them. The industry's power over a performer consisted entirely in its ability to withhold approval, and it evaporated when he stopped wanting approval, because there had never been anything else in it. He could perform without permission because performance had never required permission. It required a room, equipment, and the willingness to proceed.

The same holds here, and it holds more cleanly, because the verdict is not aesthetic. Proofs compile or they do not. The kernel accepts the term or rejects it. A corpus of formalizations covering domains the field does not value, produced outside the credentialing institutions, published without asking, is either correct or incorrect, and the mechanism that decides is not the mechanism that confers legitimacy. There is no position to be denied, no paper to be rejected, no grant to be withheld, because none was sought. The apparatus retains everything it ever actually had, which is the power to withhold its own approval.

Allin did not demonstrate that rock music ought to be dangerous. He demonstrated that it could be, that the machinery which had domesticated it had no way of stopping someone who declined to be domesticated, and that the gate had been open the whole time.

That covers what may be done without permission. For most of history there was a harder constraint, and no amount of willingness touched it: almost nobody could do this work at all. The tools were available long before there were enough people able to use them.

# AI

*Expertise is no longer scarce.*

The people in this account had drive, and so do a great many people who accomplish nothing. What separated them was equipment. Euclid had whatever it is that lets a mind see that five postulates will generate the geometry of space. Leibniz had whatever it is that lets a mind conceive of all human knowledge as amenable to calculation before any of the machinery exists. Gödel had whatever it is that lets a mind build a sentence asserting its own unprovability inside the system that contains it, and see that the construction is sound. These are not products of education, though education was required, and not products of effort, though effort was required. They were distributed by a lottery nobody entered, and the history of formal thought is substantially the history of the people who won it.

Education has never been able to fix this and has never quite admitted it. Pedagogy transmits tools. It does not transmit the equipment that makes some minds able to use the tools on problems nobody has solved. The student without it can learn the proofs and reproduce them and will not generate new ones. The student with it looks at a problem and sees structure that is not visible to anyone else in the room, and cannot explain how, because there is nothing to explain. Effort is necessary and insufficient, the deficit is biological, and no reform of the institutions was ever going to touch it.

The proof assistants did not touch it either. They moved checking from human attention to type checking, which was the whole point and was worth doing. They did nothing about generation. A proof in Coq requires knowing the system and also requires finding the argument, and finding the argument is the part that needs the equipment. Formalization costs ten to a hundred times what informal proof costs, and the multiplier is paid in human hours by people who are scarce. Voevodsky's case for formalization as a necessity collided with the fact that there were not enough people to do it, and there was no prospect of there being more.

What changed in this decade is that systems now generate proofs rather than only checking them. Networks trained on the corpus of human mathematical writing propose steps, suggest lemmas, search the space of arguments, and produce complete formalizations under thin supervision. They are not reasoning in any sense the earlier chapters would recognize. They are predicting likely continuations of formal text from statistical regularities in training data, and that this suffices to produce valid mathematics is a discovery about mathematics rather than about the systems. Nothing in the tradition from Frege to Gödel predicted that the search for proofs would turn out to be substantially a prediction problem.

The consequence for this project is narrow and total. The limiting factor on formalized knowledge was never the capacity of the proof assistants, which will check anything submitted to them and are not tired. It was the supply of people capable of producing what the assistants require. A proof checker with nothing to check is furniture. Generation required lottery winners working at human speed within human lifespans, and the field's growth rate was set by their number, which was small and not increasing. If machines generate proofs, that ceiling is gone, and the growth of formalized knowledge becomes a question of compute rather than of how many people happened to be born with the right equipment.

Every theme in the preceding chapters meets this. Llull built wheels that generated propositions faster than anyone could evaluate them, and the evaluation was the bottleneck, and his machine was useless for exactly that reason. The current situation is the inverse: the kernel evaluates faster than anything could generate, and generation has now been mechanized as well, so the pairing closes a loop that has been open since Majorca. Leibniz wanted a language in which all knowledge could be written and a procedure for deriving conclusions in it, and the language exists in fragments across the proof assistants while the procedure has become an engineering programme with measurable progress. Hilbert wanted a decision procedure for mathematics, which Gödel and Turing established cannot exist, and what has arrived instead is a generation procedure, which they did not rule out and which nobody thought to want.

Meehl's finding extends past the domains he studied. He compared clinicians against linear equations; the systems now available are not linear equations, and the structural point is untouched. Wherever human cognition is inconsistent, overconfident, and distractible, a mechanical procedure that is none of those things will win, and the advantage grows as the procedures get better. Randi's finding has a stranger descendant. A proof generated by a network may be valid and checked and entirely opaque, because there is no reasoning to inspect, only weights that fired. This opacity is not an interpretability problem awaiting a solution. It is what the method is: the systems work by capturing regularities too intricate for human inspection, and inspectability was never on offer. The verification Randi found missing is supplied by the kernel, which checks the output and does not ask where it came from. Curry and Howard identified proofs with programs, and programs are now written by systems with no concept of proof, validity, or truth, which predict text and are correct often enough to matter. That the predictions survive type checking is a fact about the structure of mathematics that the systems are exploiting without knowing there is anything to exploit.

What Coquand and Huet built is the piece that makes the exploitation possible. The kernel does not require that proofs come from something that understands them. It requires that they arrive in the right form. This is not the democratization that institutions mean when they use the word, in which the doors open a little wider while the institution retains the authority to say what knowledge is. The equipment required for discovery has stopped being a scarce resource allocated by biology, and the institutions built on the assumption that it is scarce have not noticed yet.

It does not stop at mathematics. Law, medicine, engineering, and every domain with rules that could be made explicit become tractable to people who lack the specific gifts those domains have always required. The medical protocols, the legal reasoning, the specifications that determine whether a system behaves correctly: all of it is now available to anyone with the tools and the will to point them somewhere. The sorting mechanism that allocated credentials and positions by cognitive capacity loses its grip when the capacity can be instantiated on request.

People who built their identities on that scarcity will experience this as an assault, and they will not be entirely wrong. The reverence for genius, the cult of the exceptional mind, the treatment of insight as something that cannot be analyzed, served real functions. It organized the distribution of attention and money. It gave meaning to those who had the gifts and a direction to those who hoped to acquire them. When the scarcity goes, those structures go, and what replaces them is not visible from here.

The hostility in the Kaczynski chapter will recruit. The manifesto is in circulation and the arguments will be taken up by people who experience the dissolution of cognitive scarcity as the end of themselves. When machines took livelihoods the response was Luddism, which was aimed at property. When machines take identity the response will be worse, and it is already being rehearsed affectionately online by people who have not yet decided how serious they are. This account does not assume that everyone benefits or that anyone is reconciled. It observes that the work is possible and that some people will do it whether or not the rest approve.

The tools are free and require nothing but a computer and a connection. Gatekeeping had purchase when access required institutional affiliation, a library, proximity to people who knew things, and years of credentialing, and it has none over resources that copy without limit and transmit without permission. The apparatus keeps what it always had, which is legitimacy, recognition, funding, and positions. It does not have the tools. A proof that compiles is valid whether or not its author holds a doctorate, and a formalization the kernel accepts is correct whether or not a journal has seen it.

What is required now is drive: the will to aim the tools at problems nobody is rewarding, and to keep going when no credential is coming and no institution is watching, and to produce at a scale and depth the institutions cannot match because their structure assumes a scarcity that has ended. This was always the thing that mattered, and it was hidden, because drive without the equipment produced very little and the equipment was rare enough to look like the whole story. The architecture is no longer scarce. Drive is.

The world inverts at intervals that are hard to predict and easy to identify afterwards. The carnival inversions of the medieval calendar, when the lord served at table and the fool wore the mitre, were licensed for a day and released exactly enough pressure to leave the arrangement intact. The Reformation was not licensed. Putney was not licensed, and the argument made there was buried for two hundred and fifty years and then won without a shot being fired. The revolutions of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries were not licensed. In each case an order that had appeared permanent turned out to be a set of arrangements that suited some people, maintained by habit and by force and by the absence of anyone willing to ask for its warrant. Those who benefited insisted that its dissolution meant chaos, that the many could not be trusted with what had been reserved for the few, and that the hierarchy was in the nature of things. The hierarchies went anyway. What followed was continuous with what preceded it in ways the revolutionaries had not intended and different in ways the old order could not have pictured.

This upheaval belongs to that sequence. The cognitive hierarchy that sorted minds into those which originate and those which receive, and distributed prestige and position on that basis, is coming apart. It will not come apart gently and the people it displaces will not welcome it. Euclid would not recognize the tools. He would recognize the intent.
